Why Samavesh

Many of the issues that matter most to citizens never make it onto the legislature's priority list. If politicians want to win public mandates, why does their agenda so often miss what the public needs?

In a democracy, the press amplifies public issues and pushes the legislature to set its agenda on them. Just before the coronavirus outbreak, the National Herald counted 202 primetime debates on four leading Hindi news channels. Not one was on healthcare, education, the economy or unemployment.1 Issues that are not raised nationally are easy for politicians to ignore, and reforms on them do not get the political support they need.

Samavesh aims to improve the quality and vitality of public discourse in India by building on the perspectives and experience of many people. It is an arena where public policy ideas are defined, discussed and debated, to understand an issue from several sides and arrive at a consensus.

02

The idea: don't become an echo chamber

In professional schools, scholars start discussions on their own or through the institution. These discussions challenge individual thinking and build knowledge commons. Over time, though, they turn into echo chambers. Scholars with similar training tend to repeat each other, the discussion misses the complexity that other views would bring, and it loses its point.

Samavesh started from one of these discussion groups, with one objective in its name: to not end up as an echo chamber.

How a session runs

Every week, the group meets on a Zoom call. One speaker picks a topic they are working on or have a view on, and makes a critical case for it.

7min
  1. State the problem clearly, and make the case for why the group should discuss it.
  2. Propose a way forward, or provoke the discussion with a sound argument.
  3. Leave it open enough for the audience to ask questions. The group then analyzes the issue and builds a shared understanding.

Who does what

SpeakerPicks the topic and presents the case in seven minutes.
ModeratorRuns the run-through, keeps time, keeps the room inclusive and draws the conclusion.
CoordinatorSends the housekeeping email and shares some of the moderator's duties.
CuratorTurns the recorded discussion into a publishable article.

The moderator's job

A Samavesh is informal. The moderator's job is to protect that while keeping the discussion useful.

Before

Prepare the speaker

  • Reply to the coordinator's housekeeping email to contact the speaker.
  • Hold a run-through about 24 hours before.
  • Give no personal views on the topic during the run-through. Only check that the talk is ready, within 7 minutes, clear and sound.
During

Keep it open and fair

“The moderator is the most curious person in a discussion.”
  • Keep the session informal and let ideas flow.
  • Check fallacies.
  • Balance the power dynamics between discussants.
After

Keep the record

  • Upload the recording to an unlisted YouTube channel.
  • Share the link with the Samavesh core team.
04

Rules for a good discussion

Socratic dialogues bar no one, while seminars assume some qualification in the subject. Samavesh sits in between. No one needs to be a philosopher or cite literature to take part, but an analytical and critical mindset helps. The moderator guides the group, and every participant follows six rules:

Be briefMake your point and stop. No monologues.
Keep emotion in checkDon't let feeling cloud reasoned discussion.
Keep it simpleSay it so that everyone can understand.
Ask your doubtsA question is never a lesser contribution.
ListenBe attentive to others.
Seek consensusIt is not a competition. No one is an expert, and anyone can be questioned.
Sessions follow the Chatham House Rule: what is said can be used, but not who said it.2
05

From talk to text

Discussions fade unless someone writes them down. Samavesh's publication team turned the best ones into articles, and then into a journal.

Curators wrote each paper from the recorded discussion. The publication work was new, so instead of fixed rules they followed six pointers:

No personal biasThe discussion has happened. The curator's job is to turn it into one continuous paper.
Not a transcriptWrite an article fit for a reputed journal, newspaper or policy blog.
No namesEvery speaker's identity is masked. Only the ideas matter.
Free choice of formThe writer and editor choose the style, length and audience.
Note the gapsThe conclusion can name what the session did not cover but should have.
Check the claimsDiscussants often make claims without a source. Verify them and back them up where possible.
