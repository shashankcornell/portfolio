I

Discovery

Problem discovery

Capacity is an institution's ability to perform its functions. India's 4,700+ municipalities, known as urban local bodies (ULBs), had low capacity. The government planned to raise it by training the officials who run them.

At the time, training meant sending officials to dedicated institutes. That model had three gaps:

Outdated content

Curricula were fixed and set largely by one institution.

Limited reach

Officials had to attend in person, so few could train at a time.

No evidence of impact

No one could tell whether a training improved an official's work.

Problem statement

Municipal officials across India could not keep their skills current, and the government could not tell whether training improved their work.

The government decided to build a digital platform, above all one that could deliver courses. My job was to define what it should do, and for whom.

User research

We ran about 50 interviews across municipal departments in two cities, plus interviews at the ministry, think tanks and training institutes. They shaped the first project plan.

Strategy and scoping

Product strategy

Vision

Give every municipal official in India a simple way to keep learning the skills their work requires.

Strategy: move training from occasional classes at institutes to continuous online learning, with officials also learning from each other.

Before

Fixed curriculum, few seats, no record of what changed afterwards.

→
After

Current courses on demand, shared practice across cities, and progress that can be measured.

Success metrics. Unlike institute training, NULP's impact could be measured. Each goal answered one question:

Goal 1

Capacity

Are officials getting better at their jobs?

  • Skill gain (endline minus baseline score)
  • Feedback from city leaders, states and the ministry
  • User feedback
Goal 2

Community

Are officials learning from each other?

  • Direct messages
  • Forum threads
  • Share of threads resolved
Goal 3

Growth

Is NULP reaching the officials it was built for?

  • Registered users
  • Monthly active users
  • Cities and states
  • Content partners

Strategic choices

  1. Put the learner journey first: registration, course discovery, enrollment, learning, completion and certification.
  2. Build for institutional scale: onboard many cities, departments and content partners without custom work for each.
  3. Validate before scaling: launch in a few cities, fix what fails, then expand.

Technical feasibility

Decision 1: build a new learning management system (LMS), or adapt an existing one?

FactorBuild newAdapt existing chosen
Time to launchLongerShorter
Engineering effortBuild every core featureReuse core features and customize the rest
ControlFull control of the architectureLimited by the existing architecture
MaintenanceMaintain the whole stackMaintain custom parts and stay compatible with new releases
Main riskBuilding a new systemComplexity of adapting existing code

We chose to adapt. With a tight timeline, limited resources and no in-house engineering team, speed mattered more than control. Engineering effort went only to what municipal learning needed.

Decision 2: which LMS? The government wanted to own the source code and pay no license fees, so the LMS had to be open source. After comparing several open-source options, we chose Sunbird Ed, learning infrastructure originally built for school education. It fit our resources and timeline. It also supported the government's plan to integrate NULP later with Karmayogi, the civil-service training program built on Sunbird.

What we reused and what we changed

Trade-off: Sunbird was built for school education at national scale, so it was costly to maintain at NULP's size.

Product definition

MVP question: will municipal officials learn on a digital platform at all? To answer it, the MVP shipped the core course journey, from registration to completion, and the tools partners needed to publish courses. Everything else waited for evidence from the pilot.

Prioritization. One rule ranked the backlog: anything on the learner's path from registration to completion came first.

III

Product development

UX/UI design

Goal: turn a school-oriented learning interface into one that a busy municipal officer can use to learn, resolve the issue at hand, or connect with peers. The designs below show the full product, including features added after the pilot.

Design principles

Get to the answer fast

Learning needs arrive with a short deadline. Changed: a domain-first home page, search, and as few steps as possible to content.

Bite-sized by default

Officers rarely need a full course. Changed: short modules and standalone reference documents.

Speak the officer's language

Sunbird organized content by school subject and class. Changed: content organized by urban governance domain; profiles built around municipal roles.

Make trust visible

Officials act only on credible material. Changed: the content partner shown on every course; verifiable certificates.

Information architecture

The learner journey: six steps from sign-up to a verifiable certificate

The content creation journey. Learners see only reviewed content. NULP adapted Sunbird's workspace so that each step belongs to one role:

Org adminOnboards a partner organization and adds its users, one by one or by bulk upload
Content creatorBuilds a course, or uploads a PDF, video or other resource, and submits the draft for review
Content reviewerWorks through the review queue, then publishes the content or sends it back with requested changes
Course mentorOpens a batch for learners to enroll in, either open to all or invite-only

Flagging closes the loop. Learners can flag published content, and a flag reviewer decides whether it stays up.

Process. User flows, then wireframes, then clickable Figma prototypes, tested with officials from the pilot cities before sign-off. After launch, usage data and direct feedback kept shaping the designs.

Product development

NULP was built by vendor teams. Contracts and the sprint routine were the main levers on quality, speed and stability.

The team

Contracts as product design. I scoped the work and wrote the contracts for the front-end, back-end and cloud vendors. Each ran for several years, so it had to anticipate where the product was heading, the service levels we needed, how the program could fail, and the unintended consequences to guard against.

How a sprint ran

  1. 01BacklogUser stories with acceptance criteria, ranked learner's path first
  2. 02PlanningStories agreed with the front-end, back-end and design leads, with dependencies flagged up front
  3. 03BuildVendors built on NULP's copy of Sunbird Ed (release 4.10), with automated builds and deployment
  4. 04QAEach story tested against its acceptance criteria before release
  5. 05ReviewSprint review with the team, then demos to NIUA and the ministry
  6. 06ReleaseThe cloud team promoted tested builds to production

Managing technical dependencies. Building on Sunbird saved time but brought three dependencies:

  1. Upstream compatibility. Every change to Sunbird's code had to be weighed against the cost of staying in step with future Sunbird releases.
  2. Three vendors, one product. A front-end change often needed back-end and cloud work, so we sequenced it in sprint planning instead of finding it mid-sprint.
  3. A school data model. Parts of Sunbird's data were built around boards, classes and subjects, and had to be mapped to urban governance domains.
Outcome

A working MVP on an open-source stack within six months, ready for the pilot cities.

Pilot

The pilot tested two questions before the national launch: would officials learn on a digital platform, and what would bring them back?

Pilot design

ProductThe MVP, with a small set of courses and no assessments or certificates.

Revised backlog. The reference library moved to the top and was ready before the national launch. Assessments, certificates, bite-sized modules and the simpler interface came next. Manager recommendations and peer-city review joined the backlog as new items.

V

Scaling and outcome

Launch and scaling

Growth needed both sides of the platform: officials on the demand side, and trusted content on the supply side.

The growth engine

Adoption dashboard. I built Looker Studio dashboards that tracked registrations, enrollments, completions and activity by state and city. They showed which cities needed a push and gave the ministry a live view of adoption.

Outcome. NULP passed the ministry's user target within 18 months.

Optimization

Once officials were on the platform, the job was to find where they dropped off and fix it.

What I measured. I tracked the success metrics from the strategy through a monitoring and evaluation framework. In the learner funnel, I watched enrollment, activation, course completion and retention. Engagement and drop-off signals decided which enhancement went next.

Experiments. I ran A/B tests on onboarding and engagement. The clearest was on messaging. When the forum launched, any officer could message any other anonymously, so a junior officer could message a commissioner without the commissioner knowing who it was. Senior officials disliked this and stopped engaging. I tested two alternatives:

OriginalAt launch

Anonymous messages

Any officer could message any other without saying who they were.

Senior officials stopped engaging
Variant ATested

Named messages

Every message shows who sent it.

Variant BWinner

Request to connect

The sender introduces themselves, and the recipient accepts before a chat opens.

posts and replies per thread after it went live

Request-to-connect won: officials started to interact. After it went live, posts and replies per thread rose by 140%. It is the flow NULP uses today.

Keeping the community safe. I enforced the forum's policies, reviewing and removing posts, profiles and course interactions that broke them.
