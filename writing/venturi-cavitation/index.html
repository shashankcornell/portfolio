Washing Ginger with Bubbles

Farmers lose value when ginger and turmeric leave the farm muddy and unprocessed. This is the preliminary design of a 50 kg batch machine that washes them with cavitating water jets, then boils turmeric in the same chamber.

AuthorShashank Mishra
Date21 November 2017
ContextResearch assistant, IIT Guwahati Design School
StatusProof of concept. Field testing still needed.
Abstract

Ginger and turmeric farmers often lose value when rhizomes are sold with little post-harvest processing. This paper presents a preliminary engineering design for a 50 kg batch ginger and turmeric washer-cum-atmospheric turmeric boiler. It uses a horizontal perforated rotating drum, a recirculating water loop, six hydrodynamic cavitation nozzles, a settling and filtration tank, and an atmospheric boiling arrangement for turmeric curing. The design is feasible as a proof of concept, but field validation is required for soil removal, rhizome damage, nozzle clogging, water reuse, fuel use and farmer usability.

01

The problem

Fresh rhizomes come out of the ground with soil, root hairs, stones and damaged pieces. Sold that way, they fetch a lower price.

Ginger and turmeric need different processing. Ginger mainly needs washing, then drying or curing depending on the market. Turmeric is harder. It must be washed, boiled or cured, dried and polished before it becomes a stable commodity. So one machine should not force both crops through the same process. It should offer two modes.

Ginger mode

Load→Wash→Rinse→Drain→Unload

Ginger does not need turmeric-style boiling.

Turmeric mode

Load→Wash→Rinse→Drain→Fill clean water→Boil→Drain→Unload

Boiling is done in clean water, never in dirty wash water.

The design problem, then: build a compact, affordable 50 kg batch machine for small farmers and farmer groups. It should wash muddy, irregular rhizomes well, cut manual labor, reuse water, avoid bruising or peeling, and boil turmeric safely.

02

The machine

The central idea is to replace bulky conveyor washers with a compact batch drum. The drum slowly tumbles the rhizomes while water jets from cavitating Venturi nozzles strip soil from their irregular surfaces. Dirty water drains to a settling tank, is filtered, and is pumped back to the nozzles. For turmeric, the same chamber is then filled with clean water and heated.

It must never become a pressure vessel.The lid only stops splashing. During heating, steam escapes freely through an open vent. This is the most important safety rule in the design.
03

The drum

Wet rhizomes are bulky. At an assumed bulk density of 600 kg/m³, 50 kg takes up 83 liters. The drum must not be full, or nothing tumbles, so it is sized for a 35% fill.

Vproduce = 50 / 600 = 0.0833 m³ = 83.3 L
Vdrum = 0.0833 / 0.35 = 0.238 m³ = 238 L
Selected: D = 650 mm, L = 720 mm → π(0.325)²(0.72) = 239 L

Speed matters most. If the drum spins fast enough, the rhizomes stick to the wall instead of falling. That critical speed is where centrifugal acceleration equals gravity.

04

The cavitating nozzle

A Venturi nozzle narrows, then widens. As water squeezes through the narrow throat, it speeds up and its pressure falls. If the pressure falls close to the vapor pressure of water, small vapor bubbles form. When the jet slows and pressure recovers, the bubbles collapse. Each collapse releases a sharp local shock and shear. Near a surface, this helps loosen soil from the cracks and folds of a rhizome, which a plain jet struggles to reach.

Six nozzles with a 3.5 mm throat are used. The throat size balances flow against the risk of clogging. Move the pressure drop to see how flow, jet speed, cavitation number and pump power respond.

Q = CdA√(2ΔP/ρ) with Cd = 0.85. v = Q/A. σ = (Pdownstream − Pv) / (½ρv²) with Pdownstream = 104,234 Pa and Pv = 3,170 Pa. Pump input assumes 55% efficiency. The paper's design point is 3.5 bar.

At the design point, each nozzle passes 13 L/min at 22.5 m/s, for 78 L/min in total. The Reynolds number is about 88,300, so the jet is fully turbulent. The cavitation number is about 0.40. The lower it is, the more readily the flow cavitates, and 0.40 puts the nozzle in a cavitation-prone regime. How strong the cavitation really is depends on nozzle shape, water temperature, dissolved air and the distance to the rhizome. That can only be measured.

The pump needs 455 W of hydraulic power, or 827 W at 55% efficiency. Real systems lose pressure in pipes, filters and mud, so the design selects a 2.2 kW (3 HP) pump rated for 80 to 100 L/min at 4 to 5 bar.

05

Water and filtration

A batch uses 150 L of recirculating wash water, 75 L for rinsing and, for turmeric, 180 L for boiling. If all of it is replaced every time, that is 405 L, or 8.1 L per kg. That is acceptable for a first prototype. With recirculation and filtration, the target is 3 to 5 L per kg.

Filtration is not optional. Rhizomes carry soil, stones, roots and fibers, and a 3.5 mm throat will clog quickly. The filter in front of the pump must be much finer than the throat.

1Settling tank150 to 200 L. Mud sinks to the bottom.
2Basket filterAbout 5 mm. Removable, catches stones and roots.
3Fine meshAbout 1 mm, before the pump. Mandatory.
4Sludge drainAt the lowest point, with a hatch for cleaning.
06

Boiling turmeric

Washing turns out to be the easy part. Boiling is the hard part. To cure turmeric, 180 kg of water, 50 kg of turmeric and about 75 kg of steel must be heated from 25 °C to 100 °C, then held at a boil.

Energy to heat up (88.7 MJ including a 20% loss allowance) plus a 3 kW loss for a 45-minute hold (8.1 MJ). Total useful heat: 96.8 MJ per batch.

Safety rules

  1. The boiling chamber stays vented to the atmosphere. The vent is never blocked while heating.
  2. The lid stops splashing but never seals the chamber.
  3. A low-pressure relief valve, set at 0.1 to 0.2 bar gauge.
  4. A thermal cut-off that stops heating at 105 °C.
  5. The heater does not run without enough water.
  6. Hot water drains away from the operator.
  7. Motors, pump and controls are properly earthed.
07

Throughput

A ginger batch takes about 42 minutes, so the machine can wash 400 to 500 kg in an 8-hour day. A turmeric batch takes about four times as long, almost all of it waiting for water to boil and cure.

Hatched ends show a range. Practical daily capacity from the paper: ginger 400 to 500 kg, turmeric 125 to 150 kg, turmeric with preheated water 200 to 250 kg.

Boiling is the bottleneck. The fix is to heat the next batch's water in a separate tank while the current batch washes. That cuts a turmeric cycle to 99 to 114 minutes. A later version could also use a separate steam-curing unit.

Energy tells the same story. Washing a batch uses 0.74 kWh: 0.55 kWh for the pump and 0.19 kWh for the drum motor over 15 minutes. Boiling a turmeric batch uses about 30 kWh. Boiling dominates the operating cost.

08

Testing and risks

A prototype should not be judged by whether it runs. It should be judged by numbers.

Soil removal≥ 90%Soil removed as a share of the soil on the rhizomes.
Damage rate≤ 3%Rhizomes bruised, peeled, cracked, broken or discolored.
Water use3 to 5 L/kgDown from 8.1 L/kg without reuse.
Net benefit(Pp − Pr) − CoPrice gained per kg, minus operating cost. Machine cost divided by this gives the payback.

The last metric is the point of the machine. It exists to help farmers capture more of the value of their crop.

Boiling timeAbout 62 minutes to reach a boil at 24 kW, plus 45 to 60 minutes of curing. Preheating is the first upgrade.
Nozzle cloggingMud, small stones, root hairs and fibers will block a 3.5 mm throat if filtration is weak.
Rhizome damageStrong jets, long washes or high drum speed can bruise or peel the produce. Cleaning and damage must be tested together.

The machine is feasible as a proof of concept. The washing side falls within practical ranges for pump power, nozzle flow, cavitation number and drum speed. What remains is to build it and measure it.
