Redefining Delhi's Rental Market: Addressing Homelessness through Strategic Legislative Reforms

Delhi has about 5 lakh homeless people and about 5 lakh vacant houses. A rent law from 1958 keeps the two apart. This essay explains why, and proposes a way to change the law that can survive its politics.

AuthorShashank Mishra
OriginThesis on homelessness in New Delhi, Indian School of Public Policy
Law in questionDelhi Rent Control Act, 1958
ProposalReform the law for new tenancies first, then for everyone
Abstract

This essay analyzes Delhi's housing market and the conditions of its homeless population. It argues that one way to reduce homelessness is to curb the state coercion exercised by the Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958, and so let a rental market form for the low-cost segment. It examines how the Act's intervention in the rental market has backfired, and why it must be amended in phases. To get there, it proposes a strategy that regroups the stakeholders and changes their incentives, so that legislative reform becomes possible.

01

What a house does

Cities are where people come together to trade skills, knowledge and expertise. That makes them engines of a country's economy.

A city offers a full range of workers, so production gets cheaper and more businesses become viable. Like any market, high demand for labor draws in more workers, at every skill level. Each new arrival needs resources, and housing is one of the most basic.

A house gives its resident much more than a roof:

Safety from threats and weatherSecurity for belongings and dependentsPrivacyDignityA better quality of lifePolitical bargaining powerA sense of citizenship

A house is also the unit through which a city's infrastructure reaches people: electricity lines, water pipes and sewers. And it gives the resident an address. That address is the last-mile identity for services such as phone connections and postal delivery.

Setting the legal status of property aside, a city's housing runs along a spectrum. Each step down offers fewer of these benefits, but every step still gives shelter. Having no shelter at all is homelessness.

02

Homelessness in Delhi

~5 lakhpeople homeless in Delhi1
87–90%of them are migrants1
~5 lakhhouses vacant, Census 20116
₹500average monthly saving of a homeless person7

Most migrants are not homeless. But most of the homeless are migrants.

Cities will keep growing as the economy grows. That means more demand for labor and more migrants. If Delhi cannot house people today, what will the picture be a decade from now?

Delhi's administration answered with night shelters. It set up pagoda tents on the footpaths, and special teams moved the homeless into them. The city then claimed that homelessness had fallen sharply. That claim raises two basic questions.

Question 1 · the political oneIs providing shelter the government's only job, and why should it act at all? Question 2 · the economic oneHousing does not mean owning a house. If there is demand for cheap rentals, why doesn't the market supply them?

And behind both: can a city be called developed while its citizens live on the street?

03

Why the government should act

Living without shelter hurts the homeless, and it also hurts the people around them. Public health is the clearest case. Without sanitation, people defecate in the open and waste piles up. According to The Lancet, homeless people are more exposed to both communicable and non-communicable diseases.2

“Homeless people have higher rates of premature mortality than the rest of the population, especially from suicide and unintentional injuries, and an increased prevalence of a range of infectious diseases, mental disorders, and substance misuse.”

Fazel, Geddes and Kushel, The Lancet, 20143

In homeless families, this harms the physical and mental health of children. Homeless people are also more prone to drug abuse, alcoholism and disabling conditions.4 Portillo and colleagues find that homelessness leads to severe insecurity in water, sanitation and hygiene (WaSH).5 Because the homeless live close to everyone else, communicable diseases can spread through the neighborhood.

ExternalityDisease does not stay on the footpathPoor sanitation and close quarters put the whole neighborhood at risk.
Public goodThe market has no incentivePublic health benefits everyone, so no private player is paid to provide it.
SoHomelessness is a public policy problemFixing the root cause leaves the whole city better off.

Public health is only one of several harms, but it is reason enough for the government to step in. The root cause, though, is homelessness itself, and that is what needs solving.

04

Why the market does not provide

Houses in a city are expensive to buy. A homeless migrant has neither the savings to buy one nor access to credit. That leaves two kinds of options.

Option 1Informal housingSlums and jhuggi jhopri (JJ) clusters. They sit mostly outside the city's infrastructure grid and raise their own public health concerns. This is a policy problem too, but a separate one, so the essay sets it aside.
Option 2Formal housingBuy a house with savings or a loan, or rent one. Renting is the cheapest safe option, so rental housing is the low-hanging fruit.

So look at Delhi's rental market for the poor. Supply exists: the 2011 Census counted about 5 lakh vacant houses in Delhi.6 A vacant house earns its owner nothing, so the owner should want to rent it out. Demand exists too: an IGSSS survey in 2018 found that a homeless person in Delhi saves about ₹500 a month.7 In a working market, someone would offer housing in the ₹500 bracket.

Yet there is no market in this segment. Owners act as if they are better off leaving their houses empty. When people pass up an obvious trade, something is changing their incentives. The essay argues that this something is state coercion.

05

The Rent Control Act

After Partition, Delhi took in a large refugee population. They lived in refugee camps, which later became colonies such as Lajpat Nagar, and in rented houses. As people moved out of the camps, they too looked for rented homes.

The Delhi Rent Control (DRC) Act, 1958 was passed to protect these migrants from sudden eviction. It fixes a “standard rent” for each property, so the owner cannot raise the rent as demand rises. It also protects tenants from untimely eviction.

Rent is frozenA fixed “standard rent”The owner cannot raise it to match demand.
Eviction is hardA tenant who pays staysAn owner cannot easily evict a tenant who pays on time.
Section 7(1)Raises only for renovationAnd then only by 7.5% of the cost of the work.8

Rent control changes how owners behave. In a free market, an owner rents out a house to earn income. Under the Act, maintenance and property taxes can cost more than the rent a year brings in. The owner cannot evict the tenant or raise the rent enough to profit. So the owner does the rational thing and keeps the house empty.

In 1988 the Act was amended to remove rent ceilings above ₹3,500 a month.9 That freed the top of the market, but left the low-cost segment where it was. A house rented below ₹3,500 stays under control until its rent climbs past that line. That can take a lifetime.

Apply this to the homeless. If an owner rents a house to someone who can pay ₹500 a month, it would take about 62 years before the owner could charge more. And the tenant would be protected from eviction under the Act all along. No owner takes that deal, so the house stays empty.

06

Why reform keeps stalling

The government tried before. In 1995 the legislature passed a new Rent Control Act, but the government never notified it. Tenants already covered by the 1958 Act, who had secure housing, opposed it.

The stakeholders in a one-shot reform
Political partiesWant votes.High power
Property ownersWant higher rents, so they support reform.Few voters
TenantsDon't want rents to rise, so they oppose it.Many voters

Tenants far outnumber owners, and as residents of Delhi they carry more weight at the ballot box. Reform of the low-rent segment is clearly needed, but its political economy is hard. No party wants to go against the majority by changing the law. That is why every attempt since 1995 has failed.

The need for reform is clear. The way out is a strategy that avoids the political deadlock.

07

Split the stakeholders

The essay's key move is to regroup the stakeholders. Instead of three blocs, there are five groups with different interests.

Group 1Political partiesWant votes.
Group 2Owners who already rent outWant the law amended completely.
Group 3Owners who don't rent, because of the ActWant market rent, which the Act does not allow.Future market
Group 4Tenants under the Act todayWant no change to the status quo.Left untouched
Group 5Homeless peopleSave about ₹500 a month and want rental housing within it.Future market

Reforming the whole market at once will not win a mandate. So leave Group 4, the people who want nothing to change, exactly as they are. Change the law only for the future market: new tenancies. That market brings together Group 5, the homeless with about ₹500 a month, and Group 3, the owners who hold back because of the Act.

Phase 1 · now

New tenancies only

Amend the Act so it does not apply to new rental contracts. Existing tenants keep every protection they have, so the majority has nothing to oppose.
Phase 2 · later

Retire the old law

As the homeless find homes and more tenants pay market rent, supply and demand grow. Most tenants will then have no reason to block reform, and the whole Act can be amended.
08

A logical framework for change

The essay ends with a logical framework. It runs from what the government does to what changes, with the assumptions each step depends on.

Activities
Amend the DRC Act, 1958 for the future market only.
Set up an “ease of doing business” committee for related reforms, such as floor area ratio rules and lower registration duties.
Outputs
AssumesThe market will build new low-cost housing for the ₹500 segment.
Owners sign legal contracts and rent their vacant houses to the homeless.
Builders build taller, to earn more from scarce, costly land.
Outcomes
AssumesThe homeless find homes and choose to stay in the city, even in old age.
The average age of the homeless population rises.
The health burden falls.
Rents come down as the market reaches equilibrium.
Impact
AssumesMoving from the street into a home raises a person's income and economic activity.
Trust in the government rises.
Household income and economic activity rise.

The argument, in short: Delhi does not lack houses or people who need them. It lacks a market that lets them meet. Free that market for new tenancies, and the city can start to house its homeless without asking any current tenant to give anything up.
