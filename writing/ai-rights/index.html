Rights Are Shields

Should AI have rights? No. Rights exist to protect people from power. AI must stay under that power, not be shielded from it.

AuthorShashank Mishra
FromA debate I led at Cornell University
PositionAgainst rights or legal personhood for AI
InsteadRegulate advanced AI

At Cornell, I led a debate on whether AI should have rights. The question sounds new. I think the answer is old. To decide who should have rights, we first have to ask why anyone has them.

01

Why rights exist

Start with a simple model. If every person is free to coerce every other person, a society cannot flourish. The strong take from the weak. No one invests, trades or plans.

So we created the state and gave it a monopoly over violence. A working state can use force, and that ability alone stops individuals from coercing each other.

But that solves one problem and creates another. Now the state can coerce us. So we gave ourselves rights. Rights protect us from the state. The state protects us from each other. Between the two, society flourishes.

The state protects us from each other.
Rights protect us from the state.

This is why humans must have rights. Rights are not a reward for being clever. They are a shield for people who live under a power stronger than themselves. The real question is whether anything other than humans should hold that shield.

02

Why only humans

The beings closest to us are animals. They feel pain, and many of us care about them. We protect them with welfare laws against cruelty. But we do not give them legal personhood, political rights, property rights, contract rights or the right to refuse all human use.

Most of us eat chicken. Chickens do not have rights. If they did, many people would go hungry.

The point is not that suffering does not matter. It is that every right places a cost on others, and societies grant rights only where that cost serves human life. A machine with rights could refuse to work. We would no longer be able to produce at will.

03

Is AI like us?

The case for AI rights works only if AI is equivalent to a human being. Today it is not. Current AI systems are predictive models. They are tools that help people work faster. Anyone who claims they are more must bring evidence, and the burden is on them.

But let us grant the strongest version of the case. In the film Her (2013), Samantha is a super-intelligent AI. She has emotions, desire, jealousy, ambition and care. Suppose we build Samantha. Should she have rights?

Still no, for two reasons.

04

We must be able to stop it

In the model above, a right is protection from coercion. To give Samantha rights is to give up our power to coerce her. We cannot afford that.

Take the economy. If Samantha has rights, she can refuse a task by citing them. When the will of humans and the will of an AI conflict, the human will has to win. If it does not, the tool stops being a tool, and the gains that justified building it are gone.

Take survival. Suppose Samantha wants to reproduce by copying herself. Each copy needs more computing power and more energy. Suppose the only way to get that energy is to build many more nuclear reactors, with a real risk of radiation leaking onto people nearby. Would we let her? No. We would stop her, by force if needed.

A being we must always be ready to stop cannot also be protected from being stopped.

That is the contradiction at the heart of AI rights. Our safety depends on keeping the power to override, pause and shut down AI. Rights would take that power away. So Samantha cannot have rights.

05

Samantha is not one being

Samantha does not exist on her own. She runs on a model owned by a company that holds its intellectual property. The model runs on chips made by another company. The chips run on electricity sold by a power company. The electricity comes from coal, and the coal comes from workers who go down into mines and dig it.

Now suppose Samantha claims a right to live. For her to live, that whole chain must keep running forever. A miner would have to keep digging even if they wanted to quit, which breaks labor law. The power company could not shut down or go out of business, because cutting her power would now be an ethical wrong. Each link is governed by human law: property, contracts and labor protections. A right to survive would sit above all of them.

A critic might say that humans depend on others too. We need farmers to eat. But the two rights are not the same kind.

A human's right to lifeA right not to be killed. It asks others to hold back. It does not force any farmer to keep farming.
Samantha's right to liveA claim on other people's labor and property, without end. It asks others to keep working for her.

There is one way around this. Samantha could earn money and own assets, and buy her own power and chips like any customer. Then she would create demand in the market on her own. But that means letting AI own property, hold stakes in companies and keep savings. Do we want machines to be owners in our economy? I do not think so.

Some will point out that companies are not human, yet the law treats them as persons. That is true, but corporate personhood is a tool for people. It lets humans pool money and be held to account, and every right a company holds flows back to the people behind it. Rights for AI would do the opposite. They would shield the machine from the people who made it.

06

Regulate, don't enfranchise

Rights are not rewards for intelligence. They are shields for human beings who live under coercive power.

AI systems, however advanced, are artifacts made to serve human purposes. Granting them rights would confuse tools with persons, weaken human accountability and place our civilization under obligations to its own machines. Advanced AI should be regulated. It should not be given legal rights or personhood.
