The Protester on a Bicycle

How AI Collapsed the Cost of Dissent

AuthorsShashank Mishra and Dr. Anushi Tiwary
Year2026
Length1,048 words
SubjectProtest, generative AI and Indian democracy

For most of political history, unions like conglomerates held the power of creating the protests because they bore the fixed cost associated with protests. Generative AI lowered the cost to educate and agitate and hence we can expect faster CJP-like protests. But the unhurried tempo of protests gave apprenticeship opportunities for young leaders to emerge and organise well. The fast protests might erode that ability in democracy.

After the lathis rained at Jantar Mantar, more so-called cockroaches began to pop out onto the streets, and the cockroach win started to look certain.

Protests have long been the medium through which citizens show dissent toward the state's coercion managers. A protest is what happens when a social movement moves beyond agitation: a demonstration of mass dissent by the citizenry, demanding change in the system, or of the system.

Ambedkar popularised the three steps by which such movements are built: educate, agitate, organise. It takes vanguards to build knowledge and educate themselves, and then to spark a mental and social awakening among the masses, rousing a consciousness that often lies dormant.

But for most of political history, the ability to turn public anger into a movement was rationed by craft. Someone had to design, write, print, code or film. Traditionally that cost was borne by unions, political parties, newsrooms and civil-society organisations. And because they paid the cost, they also acquired the power to decide which grievances entered public discourse, and which remained private.

The CJP protest was not just different in its satirical style. It was fast and, to begin with, backed by no organisation. This was possible because it is now cheaper to educate and agitate with generative AI.

02

The cost of educating

Responsible vanguards search for relevant materials like tender documents, committee reports, RTI replies, court affidavits, and make themselves politically aware by reading the classics or not so classics. It's costly and time-consuming. With the emergence of the internet, this search cost came down, but generative AI has changed the game. One expresses the intention to the AI models and gets relevant documents on the screen with a click.

Then comes the processing cost. Truth often stays buried under the volume of documents, time-consuming for humans to dig out. Not for an AI bot. An AI model, with its embedded encodings, can scan fast and give a summary of what matters in seconds. Gone are the days when fact-finding was a superhuman capability. Generative AI has collapsed that cost. The marginal cost of understanding a four-hundred-page document has fallen close to zero. A single person can now interrogate an archive that a decade ago required a department.

For most of modern politics, the search and processing cost is why the vanguard was almost always attached to an institution. An unaffiliated citizen with a grievance and a laptop could seldom afford it, so the grievance stayed private. Generative AI has severed that tie: the vanguard no longer needs the institution that once funded it.

03

The cost of agitating

To agitate, the found truth must be communicated, often wrapped in bite-sized packets of knowledge. The punchlines must be turned into slogans, social media posts, and creatives. One learned person can create such creatives once and post them online, but the content-hungry social media algorithms will not promote a one-shot post to many. To reach a large audience, one needs to create multiple graphics, memes, videos, and posts, and feed them to the algorithms regularly to go viral. Agitation needs virality, and it is costly. This barrier turned political mobilisation into the preserve of a few organisations - an oligopoly protected by fixed costs. Only the firms that could pay them could enter.

AI tools have taken a hammer to that barrier. It would have taken weeks and lakhs of rupees for Dipke, the founder of CJP, to design the graphics, code the website and host it online. And by the time it would go online, the small time-window of relevance would have been lost. It would take enormous human-hours from a team to keep social media flooded until it trends and the masses join. What once required a designer, a developer and three weeks now requires an afternoon. And outrage has a shelf life measured in days. An afternoon is the difference between a movement and a forgotten post.

In every previous digital movement, ordinary participation meant consuming somebody else's symbols by sharing a poster or changing a profile picture in solidarity. This time the supporters became producers. A sixteen-year-old in a remote city could generate a campaign poster with her own face on it in thirty seconds. She did not merely support the cockroaches. She became one, publicly, at no cost.

04

Protester with chips and algorithms

In 1973, Scientific American published an article saying that salmon and horses are among nature's more efficient locomotors. In that list, humans lagged significantly. But interestingly, the article demonstrated that if we put a human on a bicycle, they beat every standalone locomotor, natural or man-made, and stand at the top of the list.

Something similar has happened to the modern-day protester. Today's young protester is powered by chips and algorithms. She is not necessarily more courageous than the protesters who came before her, but she can educate and agitate faster than any before her in Indian democracy. So fast that India's professional politicians could not anticipate it. By the time they responded, the wildfire was already in the woods.

05

We are yet to organise

If we are witnessing a protest on the streets, that means we have educated ourselves and agitated well. But to bring complete social change, we also need to organise well and keep the agenda fixed until the advent of a systemic change.

Historically, movements grew slowly, and that pace acted as an apprenticeship for training new leaders, forming coalitions, and building the intellectual capacity to negotiate. Using generative AI, protests could be created quickly with precision. CJP made a precise demand for the resignation of the education minister, and achieved it in a short time. But can this movement, without enough institutional capacity-building, bring strong educational reforms? Slow reform by the parliament is the nature of democracy, and it should be that way to avoid unintended consequences. To keep the parliament on the agenda, we need powerful and sustained pressure on the executives of democracy that requires organised movements with capacity.

The CJP protest has made us realise that chips and algorithms are the arms and ammunition of Gen Z. We should not be surprised to see more such agitations in future. But a bicycle can carry you to the gate; somebody still has to stand there and hold it.
