The paper

Cities cause most of the world's emissions and will bear most of the damage. Yet climate finance rarely reaches them.

Urban 20 (U20) is the G20's group of cities. During India's G20 Presidency in 2023, it published this white paper to identify the critical levers for getting more climate finance to cities, especially in developing countries, and to recommend a way forward. It was written by an NIUA research team with a task force from organizations including Climate Group, the UK's FCDO, AFD, the Climate Bonds Initiative, C40 Cities and IEEFA.

~75%of global carbon emissions come from cities
~70%of the world's people will live in cities by 2050
$29.4tninvestment potential in urban climate projects by 2030 (IFC)
4%of the 500 largest cities in developing countries are creditworthy internationally

The promise and the shortfall

In 2009, developed countries promised USD 100 billion a year by 2020 for climate action in developing countries. In 2020 they provided USD 83.3 billion, and most of it went to mitigation rather than adaptation.

The paper names three barriers: little awareness of the returns from climate projects, inconsistent policies that leave investors unsure, and limited technical capacity in cities. It also stresses that every country needs its own approach, since cities differ in what they are allowed to borrow and how they are governed.

Chapter 3: Key enablers

Finance exists. Cities are rarely ready to receive it.

The chapter starts from one observation: accessing climate finance takes institutional tools, mechanisms and capacity at the national, provincial and local level. Private and market funding will not work for everything. Storm water, heat and flood management and other local public goods may need public money. PPPs suit some areas, such as street lighting and EV charging, but should not be the only option. So countries need a broad national framework that can draw on many kinds of finance, depending on the project.

Eight elements of a national framework

AInstitutionsClear governance, policy and coordination across agencies.
BPolicyBy-laws, tax incentives and clear signals for investors.
CCapacityTechnical skill to find, design and monitor projects.
DFinancesTransparent management that keeps cities eligible.
EPipelineBankable green projects, and the ones that are not.
FDataEmission inventories, risk assessments, monitoring.
GPartnershipsWith funders, institutions and other cities.
HCommunityLocal consent that makes projects last.

3.1

De-risking climate investments

Investors see urban infrastructure as a high-risk sector, and climate projects as riskier still. Addressing those risks is the first step to attracting finance.

Why investors see high risk

  1. Projects cut across energy, transport, waste and water.
  2. Policies and regulations are inconsistent or unclear.
  3. High upfront costs, long paybacks, uncertain revenue.
  4. Floods, heatwaves and extreme weather threaten the assets.
  5. Local resistance and conflicting interests.
  6. Weak city governance raises legal and contract risk.

What reduces it

  1. Stable policy and regulation with long-term targets.
  2. Thorough risk assessment and risk transfer.
  3. Blended finance, guarantees, green bonds, insurance.
  4. Better project preparation and structuring.
  5. Stronger local government capacity.
  6. Reliable, shared data on risks and performance.
  7. Public-private partnerships with clear roles.
  8. Knowledge sharing among stakeholders.
  9. Demonstration and pilot projects.
  10. Long-term planning for steady project pipelines.
3.2

Developing a taxonomy of climate projects

A taxonomy defines which investments count as green. Without one, money cannot be steered with confidence, and projects can be passed off as greener than they are, which is greenwashing. Most countries are only starting to build theirs. The chapter argues that each should fit its local context, including how far cities can borrow on their own, and lists the World Bank's common principles for building one.

ADefine the strategic goal.
BSelect environmental objectives that match national priorities.
CSpecify the sectors expected to deliver them.
DSelect investments by their expected performance against national targets.
EIdentify users and beneficiaries, and their roles.
FOutline reporting guidelines for market actors.

Cities have a particular opening here. In India, the 12th Schedule of the Constitution gives municipalities 18 functions, and many of them overlap with the sectors that multilateral development banks already treat as green.

3.3

Creating a country-level climate information architecture

Knowledge about climate initiatives in cities is scattered and hard to use, which leads to overlap and waste. Where national frameworks exist, they rarely reach down to cities, so no one can tell whether cities are on track with national targets. The chapter proposes a network of institutions, a hub and spoke model, that captures and shares this knowledge.

  • AA knowledge hub on funding sources and mechanisms.
  • BCapacity to capture and clean data on low-carbon assets.
  • CTechnical help to develop bankable projects.
  • DTraining on project preparation and climate funds.
  • EInput to policy and advocacy.
  • FPartnerships with banks, funders and civil society.
  • GResearch on new financing models.
  • HMonitoring and evaluation of what works.
  • IExchange with similar institutions worldwide.
3.4

Enhancing the creditworthiness of cities

Low revenue and weak governance keep most cities from borrowing, so they depend on grants from higher governments. World Bank estimates show how few of the 500 largest cities in developing countries can borrow at all.

4%creditworthy in international markets
20%creditworthy in local markets

Better credit helps everyone involved. Lenders can price risk. Cities can get lower interest on green loans, and can plan how to improve their rating. National governments set the conditions and run regular assessments, while cities fix their accounting and financial management. The chapter points to programs that already help, such as the World Bank City Creditworthiness Initiative, the C40 Creditworthiness Academy and the IDB's Emerging and Sustainable Cities Program, and suggests green credit ratings for cities.

3.5

Adopting an environmental, social and governance framework

Many investors now run funds dedicated to ESG. Cities that build systems to address these concerns can reach that money and earn better green credit ratings. The chapter sets out actions across a city government's three core roles.

AspectRegulationsStrategic planningService provision
EnvironmentalRules that cut emissions in mobility, energy, water and waste.Net zero in master plans: transit-oriented and compact development, net zero buildings, green-blue infrastructure.Cleaner technology, nature-based solutions, circular waste and wastewater, low-carbon mobility.
SocialPolicies for social cohesion and the inclusion of vulnerable groups.Social issues in local plans, universal accessibility, equal voice in planning.Adequate and inclusive municipal services for everyone.
GovernancePartnerships across government, civil society and the private sector; transparent decisions and budgets; efficient allocation of funds; responsible procurement; oversight of environmental and social performance.
3.6

Identifying a pipeline of green projects

A city needs a climate action strategy backed by a pipeline of bankable projects, gathered into a Climate Investment Plan. Core services such as water, waste and wastewater hold large potential to cut emissions, for example by capturing methane or electrifying public fleets. Private money will only follow projects with adequate returns, so the plan should bundle bankable and non-bankable projects together. Some cities in G20 countries have already raised money this way.

3.7

Exploring innovative climate financing options

Green bonds

Bonds whose proceeds fund only environmental projects. Cities can build a bond strategy around their creditworthiness and follow ICMA's Green Bond Principles. Linking projects to bonds also improves transparency and financial management.

Transition finance

Funding for hard-to-abate sectors as they decarbonize over time. The G20 Sustainable Finance Working Group's 2022 framework has five pillars:
  1. Identify transition activities
  2. Report on them
  3. Develop financial instruments
  4. Design policy measures
  5. Assess and reduce social and economic harm

Climate risk insurance

Public assets are usually uninsured. Disasters and poor infrastructure cost households and firms in low- and middle-income countries about USD 390 billion a year.
IndemnityPays on the actual loss. Costly and complex for cities.ParametricPays a set amount when a trigger, such as wind speed, is met. Better suited to cities.
