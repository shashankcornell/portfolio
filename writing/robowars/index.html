Design of a Six-Wheeled Wedge Robot

Most fighting robots carry a spinning blade or a hammer. This one had no weapon at all. It was a 24 kg, six-wheeled steel wedge, designed to get under an opponent and push it out of the way.

TeamShashank Mishra and Vishal Singh
Year2013
Built inCollege workshop. Designed in SolidWorks.
StrategyDefensive wedge and pusher. No active weapon.
Abstract

This paper presents the design, fabrication and engineering analysis of a six-wheeled heavyweight robot built for Robowar. The robot weighed about 24 kg and was designed as a defensive wedge and pushing robot rather than an active-weapon robot. It used a mild steel TIG-welded chassis, custom lathe-machined metal wheels with rubber contact surfaces, a chain-and-sprocket drivetrain and a two-motor skid-steer drive. The aim was to maximize traction, frontal stability, pushing force and structural durability.

Original title: Design and Fabrication of a 24 kg Six-Wheeled Defensive Wedge Robot for Robowar Applications

01

The idea

A Robowar robot has to survive hits, grip the floor, and stay in control while something heavy pushes back.

Weapons are hard to build, and they break. So we put everything into the parts that decide a pushing match: ground contact, torque, wedge geometry and a strong chassis. The plan was simple. Get the wedge under the opponent, lift its front off the floor so its wheels lose grip, and drive it where we wanted.

Push hardMaximize pushing force through traction.
Stay plantedSix wheels for stable ground contact.
Get underA low front wedge to lift opponents.
Take hitsA mild steel chassis that absorbs impact.

Two more goals shaped every choice: it had to be buildable in a college workshop, and it had to stay controllable while accelerating, turning and pushing.

02

The layout

Three wheels sit on each side. One DC geared motor drives each side, and a chain carries its power to all three wheels on that side. The two sides are controlled separately, so the robot can drive forward, reverse, curve, or spin in place.

03

Why six wheels

A pusher is only as good as its grip. The robot weighs 24 × 9.81 = 235 N. Spreading that over more wheels keeps more of it on the floor when the robot is hit or when one wheel lifts.

Load on each wheel 39.2N
GainsGrip and stabilityMore contact points, better load spread, steadier under impact, and no total loss of traction if one wheel lifts. It also suits a chain running along each side.
CostScrub when turningIn a skid-steer turn the wheels must slide sideways, and more wheels means more scrub. We accepted this. Pushing mattered more than nimble turning.
04

Chassis and wedge

The chassis was mild steel, TIG welded. Mild steel is strong, tough under impact, easy to find and easy to weld. The frame had to take direct collisions, twisting loads when turning, and concentrated loads at the wedge.

The wedge did three jobs. It slid under opponents and turned forward motion into lift. It lowered the robot's front profile. And it shielded the drivetrain and electronics behind it.

A 150 mm wedge at 30° rises to 86.6 mm. That is low enough to slip under most robots, and deep enough to be built as a welded steel structure.

05

How hard it pushes

A wheeled robot cannot push harder than its tires can grip. Beyond that, the wheels spin. So the drivetrain was sized backward from the friction limit, not forward from the motors.

The safety factor of 2.5 covers acceleration, chain losses, bearing friction, impacts and uneven loads on the wheels. Rolling resistance, by contrast, is tiny: 0.03 × 235 = 7 N, about one twentieth of the pushing limit. The drivetrain was built for fighting, not cruising.

06

Speed, power and gearing

The target speed was 1.2 m/s, fast enough to close distance in a match and slow enough to control. A 125 mm wheel travels 0.393 m per turn, so it must spin at 1.2 / 0.393 = 3.05 turns per second, about 180 rpm.

The motors ran at 300 rpm. A 12-tooth sprocket on the motor driving a 20-tooth sprocket on the wheels gives a ratio of 20 / 12 = 1.67. That brings 300 rpm down to 180 rpm and raises torque by the same factor.

Pushing at the friction limit at full speed takes 153 × 1.2 = 184 W at the wheels, or 245 W from the motors at 75% drivetrain efficiency. At 24 V, that is about 10 A. Starts, stalls, sudden reversals and hits draw far more, so the drive was sized for 300 to 400 W and the electrics for 25 to 40 A peaks.

07

Turning

The robot steers like a tank. Run both sides at the same speed and it goes straight. Slow one side and it curves. Run one side forward and the other backward and it spins in place.

Turning moment, in-place spin 42N·m Mz = Fside × B = 76.5 N × 0.55 m. Each side pushes with up to 76.5 N in opposite directions, 0.55 m apart. That twist is what lets the robot swing its wedge toward an opponent, though six wheels scrubbing sideways resist it.
08

Building it

Everything was made in the college workshop.

2Cut and weldMild steel, TIG welded
3DrillMotor, shaft and bearing mounts
4Turn wheelsOn the lathe
5AssembleSprockets, chains, grinding, finishing
6Wire and testElectrics, controls, troubleshooting

The wheels deserve a note. We turned them on a lathe from metal for strength and accuracy, then added a rubber contact layer for grip. Making them ourselves meant the hubs fit our shafts and sprockets exactly, and they held up under pushing and impact. Since pushing depends on traction, the wheels were the heart of the design. TIG welding gave strong, clean joints for a frame that would be hit again and again.

Every number points the same way. The robot was designed around traction-limited pushing, and the drivetrain, the six wheels, the wedge and the steel frame all serve that goal. It was a lesson in the whole chain of mechanical design: CAD, drivetrain sizing, welding, machining, wiring and, finally, driving.
