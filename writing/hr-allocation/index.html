Human Resource Allocation for Capacity Building Reforms

How do you divide a small team of fellows across 36 states and union territories so that each state gets help in proportion to the work it has? A simple weighted index, built on India's list of 4,743 urban local bodies.

AuthorShashank Mishra
ContextNational Urban Digital Mission, Centre for Digital Governance, NIUA
Data4,743 urban local bodies in 36 states and union territories
MethodWeighted index by type of urban local body
Abstract

The National Urban Digital Mission set out to deploy UPYOG, a digital public infrastructure for cities built by the National Institute of Urban Affairs, across India. Rolling it out needed someone in every state to coordinate with the state government and its cities. The Mission hired a limited number of fellows for this role. This paper proposes a way to distribute them. Each state's coordination load is estimated with an index that counts its urban local bodies, weighted by type: a municipal corporation counts as 1, a municipality as 0.5 and a town panchayat as 0.1. Fellows are then allocated in proportion to each state's share of the index. A second weighting, closer to population, gives almost the same ranking of states.

Background

Under the National Urban Digital Mission, the Centre for Digital Governance was implementing UPYOG, a digital public infrastructure for urban governance built by the National Institute of Urban Affairs. It was meant to be adopted across India. A national platform, however, is only adopted state by state. Each state decides, and each state's cities have to be brought on board.

CentreMinistry and the MissionSet the direction and build the platform.
BridgeNUDM fellowOne point of contact placed in the state.
StateState governmentAgrees, plans and leads the rollout.
CitiesUrban local bodiesAdopt the platform and use it.

To bridge the centre and the states, the Mission hired NUDM fellows. Each fellow would be placed in a state and drive the rollout from there.

ConveneArrange meetings between the state, its cities and the Mission.
DeployHelp with the technical deployment of the platform.
UnblockFind and clear the bottlenecks that stall adoption.
ConnectBe the state's point of contact with the central ministry.
02

The allocation problem

There were far fewer fellows than the work required, and the work was not spread evenly.

India has 36 states and union territories, but they differ enormously. Uttar Pradesh has 750 urban local bodies. Chandigarh has one. Giving every state the same number of fellows would leave the largest states stretched thin and the smallest over-served.

Counting cities alone is not the answer either. A large municipal corporation and a small town panchayat both count as one, yet the corporation has far more departments, services and people to bring on board. A fair allocation needs to reflect how much coordination each state's cities actually demand.

03

Data

The model starts from the national master list of urban local bodies, with each body's LGD code, type, number of wards and state. After cleaning, the list holds 4,743 urban local bodies. These were grouped into three types.

Type 1243Municipal corporationsThe largest cities, with the most departments and services.weight 1
Type 22,011MunicipalitiesMid-sized towns and cities.weight 0.5
Type 32,489Town panchayatsSmall towns in transition from rural to urban.weight 0.1

Some states use other names for their urban bodies. To keep three types, they were grouped as follows.

1Karnataka's city municipal councils and town municipal councils are counted as municipalities.
2The NCT Municipal Council in Delhi is counted as a municipality.
3Notified area councils are added to town panchayats, to keep the index simple.
04

The model

Each state gets a score, T, that stands for its coordination load. Each type of urban local body adds to the score according to its weight.

Ts = 1 × MCs + 0.5 × Ms + 0.1 × TPs
Fellowss = N × Ts ÷ ΣT
MC, M, TP
The number of municipal corporations, municipalities and town panchayats in state s.
T
The state's index score. Across India, ΣT = 1,497.4.
N
The number of fellows available.

The weights set how much each type counts. One municipal corporation counts as much as two municipalities, or ten town panchayats. The index therefore rewards states with many large cities, where a rollout has the most to coordinate, without ignoring states with many small towns.

Uttar Pradesh

1 × 20 + 0.5 × 199 + 0.1 × 531
= 20 + 99.5 + 53.1
= 172.6, or 11.5% of India
Most urban bodies in the country, but most are small, so its share falls from 15.8% of all bodies to 11.5%.

Maharashtra

1 × 27 + 0.5 × 241 + 0.1 × 131
= 27 + 120.5 + 13.1
= 160.6, or 10.7% of India
The most municipal corporations of any state, so its share rises from 8.4% of all bodies to 10.7%.
05

Results

The chart shows each state's share of the index, which is its share of the fellows. The orange mark shows what its share would be if every urban body counted the same.

The weighting changes the picture in a clear way. States with many small town panchayats, such as Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Rajasthan, get a smaller share than a plain count would give them. States whose urban bodies are mostly municipalities and corporations, such as Gujarat, Telangana, West Bengal and Kerala, get a larger one.

Whole fellows are assigned by the largest-remainder method: each state first gets the whole part of its share, and the fellows left over go to the states with the largest fractions. This rounding step was added for this page.

06

A second weighting

The weights are a judgment, so the model was also run with a second set that leans much harder on large cities: 5 for a municipal corporation, 0.4 for a municipality and 0.12 for a town panchayat. This version is closer to how population is spread across the three types.

Main index

1 · MC + 0.5 · M + 0.1 · TP

    Population-leaning index

    5 · MC + 0.4 · M + 0.12 · TP

      Nine of the top ten states are the same under both. The order shifts a little, and Andhra Pradesh, with many corporations, enters the top ten in place of West Bengal. The allocation is not sensitive to the exact weights, which makes it easier to defend.

      07

      Limits

      The index is deliberately simple, so it can be explained in a meeting and checked by anyone with the list of urban bodies. That simplicity has costs.

      The weights are set by judgmentThey reflect relative workload, not a measured one. The second weighting shows the result holds, but the numbers could be refined.
      Type is a proxyTwo municipalities can differ a lot in size and capacity. Ward counts or population would sharpen the index.
      Readiness is not countedA state that has already started adoption may need less help than its size suggests.
      Small states round to zeroMany smaller states and union territories get less than one fellow. One fellow could cover a group of neighboring states.
