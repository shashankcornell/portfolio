Modeling the Inverted Pendulum and the Unicycle

Two classic problems in control and robotics. One cannot stay upright on its own. The other cannot move sideways. Their equations show exactly why, and what a controller has to do about it.

AuthorsShashank Mishra and Vishal Singh
Presented atTechnical Symposium, Inceptum 2016, GBPEC Delhi
AwardSecond prize
This versionTechnical note reconstructed from the presentation
Abstract

This note reconstructs the core mathematics of a presentation at the technical symposium of Inceptum 2016 at GBPEC Delhi. It covers two classical problems. The inverted pendulum is an unstable, nonlinear, underactuated system used to study balance and feedback control. The unicycle is a nonholonomic mobile robot whose motion is constrained by rolling without sideways slip. For both, the note presents the modeling assumptions, governing equations, state-space form, linearization and control interpretation.

01

Two classic problems

Many real machines are nonlinear, have fewer controls than ways to move, and are bound by rules about how they can move. Two small models capture all of this.

DynamicsThe inverted pendulumA normal pendulum hangs down and is stable. Flip it upright and the smallest push makes it fall, unless a controller keeps catching it. It is the standard test for balance control, used to think about rockets, robots and self-balancing vehicles.
KinematicsThe unicycleA vehicle that can drive forward and turn, but cannot slide sideways. It is the standard abstraction for wheeled mobile robots and differential-drive robots, and the starting point for navigation and path tracking.

The aim of the presentation was to show how each system becomes a set of equations, and how those equations reveal the control problem hidden inside.

02

The cart and pendulum

A cart moves along a track. A pendulum is pinned to it at a pivot. The only control is a horizontal force F on the cart. The goal is to keep the pendulum upright while also controlling where the cart is.

NonlinearThe pendulum's motion involves sin θ and cos θ, so the equations are not straight lines.
UnderactuatedOne input, the force F, has to manage two motions: the cart sliding and the pendulum rotating.
03

Equations of motion

Newton's laws, or the Euler-Lagrange method, give two coupled equations.

Horizontal force balance of the cart and pendulum(M + m)ẍ + bẋ + mlθ cos θ − mlθ² sin θ = F(1)
Rotation of the pendulum about the pivot(I + ml²)θ − mgl sin θ = −mlẍ cos θ(2)

The two motions are tied together. Accelerating the cart (ẍ) appears in the pendulum's equation, and the pendulum's swing (θ and θ²) appears in the cart's. That coupling is the whole trick of balancing: the only way to move the pendulum is to move the cart.

04

State space and linearization

Four numbers describe the system at any instant: two positions and two velocities. Stacked together, they form the state.

Near the upright position (θ = 0, θ = 0, ẋ = 0) the angle stays small, so three approximations hold: sin θ ≈ θ, cos θ ≈ 1 and θ² ≈ 0. The equations become linear.

The highlighted term is the instability, written as a number. In the equation for θ it is positive: the more the pendulum leans, the faster it accelerates into the lean. Any small tilt grows on its own unless a force acts.

05

Balancing it

The goal is θ → 0, and if needed x → xdesired. A common approach is state feedback: measure all four states and push the cart in proportion to them.

F = −Kz ⟹ ż = (A − BK)z(15, 16)

Choosing the gain matrix K moves the eigenvalues of A − BK into the stable region, the left half of the complex plane. In plain terms, the controller drives the cart under the pendulum to catch it as it starts to fall. Try it below. Nudge the pendulum, then switch the controller off.

06

The unicycle

The unicycle lives on a flat plane. Its configuration is its position and heading, q = (x, y, θ). It has two controls: forward speed v and turning rate ω.

Kinematicsẋ = v cos θ , ẏ = v sin θ , θ = ω(18–20)
Compact formq̇ = cos θ0sin θ001vω(21)
No sideways slip−ẋ sin θ + ẏ cos θ = 0(22)

Substituting the kinematics into the constraint gives −v sin θ cos θ + v cos θ sin θ = 0, so the model never slips sideways. The constraint is on velocity, and it cannot be integrated into a rule about position alone. That is what nonholonomic means. The robot can still reach any position and heading on the plane. It just has to combine forward motion and turning to get there, as anyone who has parallel parked knows.

07

Tracking a path

To follow a reference path qr = (xr, yr, θr) moving at vr and ωr, measure the error in the robot's own frame: how far ahead (ex), how far to the side (ey), and how far off in heading (eθ).

Error in the robot's frameexeyeθ = cos θsin θ0−sin θcos θ0001xr − xyr − yθr − θ(25)
Feedback law, with positive gains kx, ky, kθv = vr cos eθ + kxex , ω = ωr + kyvrey + kθ sin eθ(26, 27)

The forward error is fixed directly by speeding up or slowing down. The sideways error cannot be fixed directly, so it goes into the turning rate instead: the robot turns toward the path, then its forward motion closes the gap. Watch it below.

08

Two models, two lessons

Inverted pendulumUnicycle
Kind of modelDynamic: forces, acceleration, gravityKinematic: position, heading, velocity
Core difficultyUnstable equilibriumNonholonomic constraint
Inputs vs. motionsOne force for two motionsTwo inputs for three coordinates
Key stepLinearize near upright, then state feedbackError in the robot's frame, then steer
LessonSome systems need active feedback just to existSome motions need planning, not just pushing

Both show the same habit of mind. Turn a physical system into equations, and the equations tell you what the controller has to do. The pendulum shows nonlinear dynamics, an unstable equilibrium, linearization and state-space control. The unicycle shows nonholonomic kinematics, motion constraints and trajectory tracking. Together they are foundational ideas in control systems and robotics.
